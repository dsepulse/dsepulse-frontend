Analysing the Bangladesh stock market

The Dhaka Stock Exchange trades for four hours a day across more than 400 listed companies. The constraint on analysing it is not a shortage of information — it is that one person cannot read all of it in the hours between the close and the next open.

The shape of the problem

Published schedules put the DSE's continuous session at 10:00 to 14:00, Sunday to Thursday, on Dhaka time, which does not observe daylight saving. That gives roughly twenty trading hours a week across a market of over 400 listed names.

For a working investor the practical consequence is specific. The data that matters — the day's range, the closing price, the volume against the stock's own average — is only complete once trading stops. Analysis of the Bangladesh market is therefore an evening activity, done against a fixed dataset, for decisions taken the following morning.

That is not a limitation to work around. It is the actual shape of the job, and a method built for it beats one borrowed from a market that trades continuously.

What can be known after the close, and what cannot

By 5:30 PM the following are settled facts for every listed stock: the day's open, high, low and close; the traded volume and how it compares with that stock's own recent average; where the price sits against its rolling 52-week range; and the direction of the DSEX as a whole.

These are not knowable from the close, and no amount of analysis produces them:

  • News that has not been published yet. A board decision, an earnings release or a regulatory action can move a share overnight regardless of what its chart said at 14:00.
  • Tomorrow's liquidity. A stock that traded heavily today may be untradeable at your size tomorrow.
  • Options-implied sentiment. The Dhaka Stock Exchange has no options market, so measures such as the put-call ratio have no inputs here at all. Any Bangladesh-market tool quoting one is quoting something that does not exist.

Breadth beats depth, on this market

A common approach is to pick ten or twenty familiar names and follow them closely. On a market this size that is an expensive habit: the opportunity is, by definition, in the stocks you are not watching.

The alternative is to apply one fixed, repeatable set of conditions to every listed name and read only what clears them. It gives up depth on any single company and buys coverage of the whole market — which, when the universe is 400 names and the analyst is one person with an evening, is the better trade.

DSE Pulse is built on that premise. Six independent engines read every listed stock after the close and publish only the names that met defined technical conditions. The full methodology sets out what each engine measures and what it cannot see.

Why one number is not enough

Any single score hides disagreement, and on this market disagreement is informative. A stock with strong momentum today but a falling long-term moving average is a bounce inside a downtrend — a different proposition from the same momentum inside an uptrend. Averaged into one figure, that distinction disappears.

Keeping the readings separate means you can see when the engines agree and when they do not, and treat the two cases differently.
