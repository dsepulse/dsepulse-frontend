DSE stock market signals, and how to read one

A signal is a statement that a defined set of conditions was true at the last close. It is not a prediction, and the difference is not pedantry — it changes what you should do with it.

Signal, prediction and recommendation are three different things

A prediction says a price will go up. Nothing on this site makes one.

A recommendation says you should buy. That requires knowing your circumstances, and no automated system knows them.

A signal says: these specific, stated conditions were satisfied by this stock at today's close. Whether that is worth acting on is a separate judgement, and it is yours.

The distinction matters because it tells you what to do when a signal does not work out. A failed prediction means the model was wrong. A signal that did not lead anywhere means the conditions were met and the outcome still went the other way — which is the expected behaviour of any probabilistic filter, and the reason position sizing exists.

How to read a published setup

Where DSE Pulse publishes levels, there are three numbers and they are derived mechanically rather than chosen.

LevelWhat it is
EntryA buy zone anchored to the session's low — a price the stock actually traded at that day, not a level it would have to gap to reach.
TargetA ladder of levels above the entry, set as fixed percentages rather than judged per stock.
StopA level below the entry at which the original condition is considered broken. On BD Signal this is derived from Average True Range rather than a flat percentage.

The stop is the important one. It is not a prediction of the worst case; it is the price at which the reason for the trade no longer holds.

Not every signal carries levels

Three of the six engines publish no entry, target or stop of their own. Tech Analysis and WMA Analysis produce a reading, not a plan; where the interface shows levels beside one of them, those levels are borrowed from an engine that does compute them and the source is named.

Smart Money borrows none at all, deliberately. It reports where unusual volume and block trades are appearing — information about where money is moving, not an instruction about where to enter.

Agreement and disagreement

The six engines run independently on the same stock, and they frequently disagree. That is the system working, not failing.

The Combined Signal exists for readers who want only the names where three engines independently clear their own thresholds on the same day. It is a much shorter list, and a much stricter one. The individual engine outputs stay visible for everyone else, because a stock that one engine likes and another does not is a specific situation worth recognising rather than averaging away.

What would make a signal stale

Every published setup is bound to the most recent completed run. If the engines do not run on a given evening, setups stop appearing rather than continuing to show levels from an earlier date.

This matters more than it sounds. A buy zone computed three months ago, presented without a date beside today's market price, is actively misleading — and it is the default behaviour of a system that stores signals without binding them to a run. See the methodology for how the binding works.
