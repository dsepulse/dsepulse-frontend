How DSE Pulse analyses the Dhaka Stock Exchange

Six engines read every listed stock after the market closes and reduce more than 400 names to a short list. This page sets out what each one measures, where the entry, target and stop come from, which data the system uses — and, just as importantly, what the method does not and cannot do.

Methodology current as of 18 November 2026

01The problem this is built for

The Dhaka Stock Exchange lists over 400 companies. A retail investor checking them by hand after work cannot read 400 charts, and the published market summary tells you what moved, not what is worth looking at tomorrow.

DSE Pulse exists to do the reading. It is a filter, not a forecaster. Each evening it applies a fixed, repeatable set of technical conditions to the whole market and publishes the names that met them, together with the price levels those conditions imply. Whether to act on any of it is the reader's decision.

02The six engines

Each engine is independent. They run in sequence after the close, each writing its own result, and they are deliberately not merged into a single number — an engine that disagrees with the others is information, not noise.

What the Daily Picker actually weighs

The Picker's score is built from a stock's move on the day, its traded volume relative to its own recent average, the direction of the DSEX as a whole, the exchange category the stock sits in, and where RSI falls. A falling market scales every score down — the same stock scores lower on a red day than a green one, because a breakout against the tide is a different thing from one with it.

The individual weights are not published. The inputs above are the complete list; there is no hidden factor, no discretionary override and no human editing of the output.

How the WMA signal is read

The signal comes from the order of the three averages, never their absolute values:

  • Strong Buy — the 9 is above the 34 and the 34 is above the 89. All three aligned upward.
  • Buy — the 9 is above the 34, but the longer average has not confirmed.
  • Sell — the 9 has fallen below the 34.
  • Strong Sell — all three aligned downward.
  • Neutral — no crossover to read.

Trend is reported separately from signal: the 34 above the 89 is an uptrend, meaningfully below it a downtrend, and the narrow band between the two is called sideways rather than forced into a direction.

03Where entry, target and stop come from

Every published setup carries three prices. They are derived mechanically, not chosen.

EntryA buy zone anchored to the session's low, not to the closing price
TargetA fixed ladder of three levels above the entry
StopA fixed percentage below the entry — or, on BD Signal, an ATR-based stop

Anchoring the entry to the session's low rather than the close matters: it means the published zone is a price the stock actually traded at that day, not a level it would have to gap to reach.

Not every engine publishes levels. Tech Analysis, WMA Analysis and Smart Money produce no entry, target or stop of their own. Where the interface shows levels beside one of those signals, they are borrowed from an engine that does compute them, and the source is named. Smart Money borrows none at all, by design — it tells you where money is moving, not where to enter.

Trading costs are excluded from every level shown

Round-trip exchange and regulatory charges on the DSE come to roughly 0.025% of turnover. Brokerage commission is on top of that and varies by broker, so it is not assumed anywhere in this system. A target that looks reachable before costs may not be after them, particularly on small positions.

04Data and schedule

Prices, volumes and the market summary are collected after each trading session from published Dhaka Stock Exchange sources. Collection runs from a connection inside Bangladesh, which matters because several of the sources behave differently for overseas traffic.

The sequence is fixed: data collection first, then the engines in the order shown above, finishing around 5:30 PM Bangladesh time. The site reflects the most recent completed run.

The rolling 52-week high and low used across the site are close-based — computed from closing prices rather than intraday extremes — and refreshed daily.

If a run is missed, setups disappear rather than go stale

Every published setup is bound to the most recent completed run. If the engines do not run on a given evening, stocks stop showing a current setup together, instead of continuing to display levels from an earlier date. This is deliberate: a three-month-old buy zone presented as today's is worse than no buy zone at all.

05What this method cannot do

Publishing the limits is part of the method. These are real, and they apply every day.

  • It is not live. Everything is computed once, after the close. Nothing updates during the trading session, and a signal published this evening describes conditions as they were at today's close.
  • It is technical only. The engines read price, volume and exchange category. They do not read news, earnings announcements, board decisions, regulatory action or anything else that moves a share overnight.
  • Illiquid stocks are excluded. Names that do not trade enough for the volume measures to mean anything are rejected rather than scored. A thin stock can produce an impressive-looking percentage move on a handful of trades.
  • Circuit limits cap what can be measured. The exchange restricts most daily moves to around 10%. A reported move beyond roughly 30% is treated as a data fault and suppressed rather than displayed, because it is far more likely to be bad data than real news.
  • There are no options-derived indicators. The Dhaka Stock Exchange has no options market, so measures such as the put-call ratio have no inputs here and are not published. Inventing one would be straightforward and dishonest.
  • Past results are not predictions. The track record reports what previously published signals went on to do. It is a record, not a forecast, and it carries no implication about any future signal.

06Why the engines are kept separate

It would be simpler to merge six scores into one headline number. The system does not, for one reason: a single number hides disagreement, and disagreement is the most useful thing a multi-engine system produces.

A stock that the Picker likes on momentum but WMA reads as a downtrend is telling you something specific — a bounce inside a falling trend. Averaged into one figure, that distinction vanishes. The Combined Signal exists for readers who want only the names where the engines agree; the individual engines remain visible for everyone else.
