Short-term trading on the Dhaka Stock Exchange

The arithmetic that decides whether short-term trading works is not the win rate. It is the cost floor — and on small positions, costs decide more trades than analysis does.

Start with what a round trip costs

Exchange and regulatory charges on the DSE come to roughly 0.025% of turnover for a round trip — buying and then selling. Brokerage commission is separate, varies between brokers, and is usually the larger of the two.

DSE Pulse assumes no commission figure anywhere, because it does not know yours. Every published target and stop is a gross level. The number you care about is net, and only you can compute it.

Work out your own floor once, then use it

Add your broker's commission to the 0.025% and double it for the round trip. That percentage is the minimum move a trade must make before you have broken even. Any setup offering less than that is not a trade regardless of how good the signal looks.

Holding period and the trading calendar

Published schedules put the DSE's session at 10:00 to 14:00, Sunday to Thursday, Dhaka time. A "short-term" position on this market typically spans a handful of those sessions rather than minutes.

That has a practical consequence: every position you hold is exposed to an overnight gap, and across a weekend, to a two-day one. Technical analysis of the close cannot see what is announced in that window. Sizing is how you survive it, not stop placement — a gap can open through a stop.

Size against the stop, not against the account

The common approach is to decide how much to invest. The more durable approach is to decide how much you are willing to lose if the setup fails, and let that determine the position size.

  1. Decide the maximum you will lose on this trade — as an amount, not a feeling.
  2. Take the distance between the entry and the stop as a percentage.
  3. Position size is the first divided by the second.

A wider stop therefore means a smaller position, automatically. That is the mechanism that stops a volatile stock quietly becoming your largest holding. The position and risk calculator does this arithmetic.

Where the stop comes from

A stop placed at a round number, or at "10% down", is unrelated to the stock. Two stocks with very different daily ranges get the same stop and the quieter one is given far more room than it needs.

DSE Pulse's BD Signal derives its stop from Average True Range — the stock's own recent daily range — so a volatile name gets a wider stop and a quiet one a tighter stop. The Daily Picker uses a fixed percentage below its entry instead, which is simpler and less adaptive; the methodology is explicit about which engine does which.

What short-term trading on this market cannot be

  • It cannot be intraday, using this data. Everything here is computed once, after the close. There is no live feed and no intraday signal.
  • It cannot ignore liquidity. A position you can enter and cannot exit is not a short-term trade. Thin stocks are excluded from the signals for this reason.
  • It cannot be high frequency. With a cost floor on every round trip, trading more often mechanically lowers your expectancy unless each trade clears that floor by a margin.
