Technical analysis on the Dhaka Stock Exchange

Most technical analysis material is written for continuously traded, deeply liquid markets with derivatives. The DSE is none of those things, and some standard indicators behave differently here — or cannot be computed at all.

Why weighted, not simple, moving averages

A simple moving average treats a price from 89 sessions ago as exactly as important as yesterday's. A weighted moving average scales the influence of each session by how recent it is.

DSE Pulse uses 9, 34 and 89 sessions, computed from 150 days of history, and reads the order of the three rather than their values:

  • 9 above 34 above 89 — all three aligned upward.
  • 9 above 34 only — the short average has turned, the long one has not confirmed.
  • 9 below 34 — the short average has rolled over.
  • All three aligned downward.

Trend is reported separately from signal, and the narrow band where the medium and long averages are effectively level is called sideways rather than forced into a direction. A market spends a lot of its time there, and saying so is more useful than picking.

Where standard indicators need care on this market

Daily price limits compress momentum

The exchange restricts how far a share can move in a session. Momentum indicators calibrated on markets without that restriction will read differently here: a move that would be extreme elsewhere may simply be the day's maximum. DSE Pulse treats a reported move beyond roughly 30% as a data fault rather than news and suppresses it, because at that magnitude bad data is far more likely than a real event.

RSI on a stock that did not move

The standard RSI formula divides by average losses. A price series with no down days at all produces a division by zero, and the usual shortcut is to return 100 — "overbought". On a thinly traded stock whose price did not change, that is exactly wrong: it reports an extreme where nothing happened. DSE Pulse returns a neutral reading in that case and reserves 100 for a series that genuinely rose without a single down day.

Indicators with no inputs here

The Dhaka Stock Exchange has no options market. Every indicator derived from options pricing — put-call ratio, implied volatility, options skew — therefore has nothing to compute from. DSE Pulse does not publish them. Inventing a proxy and labelling it with the familiar name would be straightforward and dishonest.

Technical analysis cannot see fundamentals

Everything above reads price, volume and exchange category. None of it reads an earnings announcement, a board decision, a regulatory action or a rights issue. Those move shares, and they move them overnight, between the close that produced a signal and the open where you would act on it.

A technical signal is one input. Treating it as the only one is the most common way to be surprised.
